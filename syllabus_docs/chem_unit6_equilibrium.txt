SECTION 1: CONCEPT OF EQUILIBRIUM
----------------------------------------------------------------

Dynamic Equilibrium:
→ In a closed system, when the rate of forward reaction = rate of backward reaction.
→ "Dynamic" because reactions continue in both directions — concentrations remain constant but reactions don't stop.
→ Macroscopic properties (concentration, pressure, density) remain constant.
→ Can be established from EITHER side of the equation.

Physical Equilibria:
→ Solid ⇌ Liquid: ice ⇌ water (at melting point)
→ Liquid ⇌ Gas: water ⇌ water vapour (at any temperature — but full equilibrium only in closed container)
→ Gas ⇌ Solution: CO2(g) ⇌ CO2(aq) (Henry's law)

SECTION 2: EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANTS (Kc and Kp)
----------------------------------------------------------------

For reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD

   Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^b
   (concentrations in mol/L)

   Kp = (PC)^c (PD)^d / (PA)^a (PB)^b
   (partial pressures in atm or bar)

Relationship between Kp and Kc:
   Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn
   Δn = (c+d) - (a+b) = moles of gaseous products - moles of gaseous reactants
   R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K

   If Δn = 0: Kp = Kc
   If Δn > 0: Kp > Kc
   If Δn < 0: Kp < Kc

IMPORTANT RULES FOR K:
1. K is dimensionless (activities, not concentrations strictly — but JEE uses concentration ratios)
2. K depends ONLY on temperature. NOT on concentrations, pressure, or catalyst.
3. K for reverse reaction = 1/K for forward reaction.
4. K for scaled reaction: if coefficients multiplied by n, K becomes K^n.
5. Pure solids and pure liquids do NOT appear in K expression (their activity = 1).

JEE EXAMPLE: CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g)
   Kp = PCO2 (only CO2 is gas; CaCO3 and CaO are solids → omitted)
   Kc = [CO2]

JEE TRAP: Water in aqueous solution is omitted from K expression (pure liquid).
But water in gas phase IS included.

Significance of K value:
→ K >> 1: products strongly favored at equilibrium (reaction goes nearly to completion)
→ K << 1: reactants strongly favored (barely any products formed)
→ K ≈ 1: significant amounts of both reactants and products at equilibrium

Reaction Quotient (Q):
→ Same expression as K but using NON-equilibrium concentrations.
→ Q < K: reaction proceeds forward (more products needed)
→ Q > K: reaction proceeds backward (more reactants needed)
→ Q = K: system is at equilibrium

Relationship with ΔG°:
   ΔG° = -RT ln K
   ΔG° < 0 ↔ K > 1 (products favored)
   ΔG° > 0 ↔ K < 1 (reactants favored)

SECTION 3: LE CHATELIER'S PRINCIPLE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Le Chatelier's Principle: If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to counteract the disturbance and re-establish equilibrium.

EFFECT OF VARIOUS CHANGES:

1. Change in Concentration:
   → Adding reactant → shifts forward (to consume added reactant)
   → Adding product → shifts backward (to consume added product)
   → Removing product → shifts forward (to replace removed product)
   → K doesn't change (only T changes K)

2. Change in Pressure (for gaseous reactions):
   → Increase pressure (decrease volume) → shifts toward side with FEWER moles of gas
   → Decrease pressure → shifts toward side with MORE moles of gas
   → If Δn = 0: pressure change has no effect
   → Adding inert gas at constant VOLUME: no effect (partial pressures unchanged)
   → Adding inert gas at constant PRESSURE: shifts toward more moles of gas (partial pressures decrease)

3. Change in Temperature:
   → Increase T → shifts endothermic direction (absorbs heat)
   → Decrease T → shifts exothermic direction (releases heat)
   → IMPORTANT: Temperature change DOES change the value of K.
   → Exothermic reaction: K decreases with increasing T
   → Endothermic reaction: K increases with increasing T

4. Catalyst:
   → Catalyst increases rate of BOTH forward and backward reactions equally.
   → Equilibrium is reached faster but K does NOT change.
   → Position of equilibrium does NOT shift.

JEE TRAP: Catalyst doesn't change K or equilibrium position. It only speeds up reaching equilibrium.

Haber Process Application:
N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)  ΔH = -92 kJ/mol
→ Low temperature: favors NH3 (exothermic reaction) but slow rate
→ High pressure: favors NH3 (fewer moles of gas on product side)
→ Compromise: 450-500°C temperature (moderate), 200 atm pressure, Fe catalyst
→ This is a classic JEE Le Chatelier application.

SECTION 4: IONIC EQUILIBRIUM — ACIDS AND BASES
----------------------------------------------------------------

ACID-BASE THEORIES:

Arrhenius Theory:
→ Acid: produces H+ in water. Base: produces OH- in water.
→ Limitation: only for aqueous solutions. NH3 is not a base by this definition.

Bronsted-Lowry Theory:
→ Acid: proton (H+) donor. Base: proton (H+) acceptor.
→ Every acid has a conjugate base (formed after H+ donation).
→ Every base has a conjugate acid (formed after H+ acceptance).
→ Conjugate pair differs by one H+.
→ HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl- (HCl/Cl- and H2O/H3O+ are conjugate pairs)
→ Stronger acid → weaker conjugate base

Lewis Theory (most general):
→ Acid: electron pair acceptor (Lewis acid)
→ Base: electron pair donor (Lewis base)
→ Covers reactions without H+ transfer.
→ Lewis acids: BF3 (empty orbital), AlCl3, Fe3+, Cu2+, CO2
→ Lewis bases: NH3 (lone pair), H2O, OH-, Cl-

JEE TRAP: BF3 has no H+ to donate but is a Lewis acid. F- has no H+ but is a Lewis base.

Strong vs Weak Acids/Bases:
Strong acids (essentially complete dissociation): HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4, HClO4
Strong bases (essentially complete dissociation): NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)2, Ba(OH)2
Weak acids (partial dissociation): CH3COOH, HF, H2CO3, H3PO4, HCN
Weak bases (partial dissociation): NH3, NH4OH, amines

SECTION 5: IONIZATION CONSTANTS (Ka, Kb, Kw)
----------------------------------------------------------------

For weak acid HA: HA ⇌ H+ + A-
   Ka = [H+][A-] / [HA]
   Higher Ka → stronger acid → more dissociation

For weak base BOH: B + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH-
   Kb = [BH+][OH-] / [B]
   Higher Kb → stronger base

Ionic product of water (Kw):
   H2O ⇌ H+ + OH-
   Kw = [H+][OH-] = 10^-14 at 25°C (298 K)
   [H+] = [OH-] = 10^-7 M in pure water at 25°C

Kw increases with temperature (dissociation is endothermic).
At higher temperature: Kw > 10^-14, but water is still neutral (pOH and pH change equally).

For conjugate acid-base pair:
   Ka × Kb = Kw = 10^-14
   pKa + pKb = 14

pH Scale:
   pH = -log[H+] = -log[H3O+]
   pOH = -log[OH-]
   pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C)
   
   pH < 7: acidic solution
   pH = 7: neutral solution (at 25°C)
   pH > 7: basic solution

pH Calculations:

Strong acid: pH = -log[H+] = -log[Cacid] (for monoprotic strong acid)
Strong base: pOH = -log[OH-], then pH = 14 - pOH

Weak acid (using Ka):
   [H+] = √(Ka × C) [when α << 1, i.e., < 5% dissociation]
   pH = (pKa - log C)/2 = ½(pKa + pC)
   
Degree of dissociation (α): α = √(Ka/C) [for weak acid]

JEE TRAP: pH of 10^-8 M HCl is NOT 8. You cannot ignore H+ from water for very dilute acid.
pH is slightly less than 7 (but above 7 from water's contribution). The answer is approximately 6.98.

Buffer Solutions:
Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:
   pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA])
   pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) for acidic buffer
   
   pOH = pKb + log([BH+]/[B])
   pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) for basic buffer

Buffer works best when pH ≈ pKa (when [A-] = [HA], log term = 0)
Buffer capacity: maximum around pH = pKa ± 1

JEE EXAMPLE: Acetic acid/sodium acetate buffer:
   CH3COOH/CH3COO- system, pKa = 4.74
   Best buffer capacity around pH 4.74

Common ion effect on buffer:
→ Adding common ion (like CH3COO- to acetic acid solution) suppresses ionization → pH increases.

SECTION 6: SOLUBILITY EQUILIBRIA (Ksp)
----------------------------------------------------------------

For sparingly soluble salt AxBy: AxBy(s) ⇌ xA^y+ + yB^x-
   Ksp = [A^y+]^x [B^x-]^y

Solubility Product is NOT the same as solubility.
   Solubility (s) = moles dissolved per liter

Ksp from solubility:
For AB type (like AgCl → Ag+ + Cl-):
   s = [Ag+] = [Cl-], Ksp = s²
For AB2 type (like PbCl2 → Pb2+ + 2Cl-):
   [Pb2+] = s, [Cl-] = 2s, Ksp = s(2s)² = 4s³
For A2B type (like Ag2S → 2Ag+ + S2-):
   [Ag+] = 2s, [S2-] = s, Ksp = (2s)²(s) = 4s³

Precipitation condition:
→ If ionic product (IP) > Ksp: precipitation occurs (supersaturated)
→ If IP < Ksp: no precipitation (unsaturated, more can dissolve)
→ If IP = Ksp: saturated solution (equilibrium)

Common Ion Effect on Solubility:
→ Adding a common ion decreases solubility (Q increases, equilibrium shifts backward).
→ AgCl solubility in NaCl solution << AgCl solubility in pure water
→ AgCl solubility in water > AgCl solubility in 0.1 M AgNO3

JEE TRAP: Common ion decreases solubility. This is opposite to what students might expect.

SECTION 7: HYDROLYSIS OF SALTS
----------------------------------------------------------------

Salt of strong acid + strong base: pH = 7 (NaCl, KNO3 — no hydrolysis)
Salt of weak acid + strong base: pH > 7 (CH3COONa — anion hydrolyzes, basic solution)
Salt of strong acid + weak base: pH < 7 (NH4Cl — cation hydrolyzes, acidic solution)
Salt of weak acid + weak base: depends on Ka vs Kb

For CH3COONa (sodium acetate):
   CH3COO- + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + OH-
   pH = 7 + ½(pKa + log C) [for salt of weak acid + strong base]

For NH4Cl (ammonium chloride):
   NH4+ + H2O ⇌ NH3 + H3O+
   pH = 7 - ½(pKb + log C) [for salt of strong acid + weak base]

JEE TRAP: Na2CO3 solution is basic (carbonate is from H2CO3, a weak acid).
NH4Cl solution is acidic (NH4+ is from NH3, a weak base).

SECTION 8: JEE-SPECIFIC PATTERNS
----------------------------------------------------------------

HIGH FREQUENCY QUESTION TYPES:
1. Calculate pH of weak acid/base solution using Ka or Kb
2. Calculate Kp from Kc or vice versa using Δn
3. Predict direction of reaction shift using Le Chatelier's principle
4. Calculate Ksp from solubility or solubility from Ksp
5. Apply common ion effect to solubility or ionization
6. Buffer pH calculation using Henderson-Hasselbalch
7. Identify conjugate acid-base pairs

CRITICAL TRAPS:
→ Temperature is the ONLY factor that changes K value.
→ Catalyst speeds equilibrium but doesn't change K or position.
→ Kp = Kc only when Δn = 0.
→ For NH4F (weak acid + weak base salt): need to compare Ka(HF) vs Kb(NH3).
→ Solubility from Ksp: careful with stoichiometry factor (4s³ vs s² type).
→ Buffer pH works best at pH ≈ pKa ± 1. Outside this, buffering capacity falls.

OUT OF JEE SCOPE:
→ Activity coefficients and non-ideal ionic solutions
→ Simultaneous equilibria (three or more competing equilibria)
→ Acid-base indicators (general knowledge level only, not detailed calculations)
→ Detailed derivation of Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

SECTION 9: KEY FORMULAS SUMMARY
----------------------------------------------------------------

Kc = [products] / [reactants] (using stoichiometric powers)
Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn
Ka × Kb = Kw = 10^-14 at 25°C
pKa + pKb = 14
pH + pOH = 14
[H+] = √(Ka × C) for weak acid
pH = ½(pKa - log C)
pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]) (Henderson-Hasselbalch)
For AB: Ksp = s², s = √Ksp
For AB2 or A2B: Ksp = 4s³, s = (Ksp/4)^(1/3)
