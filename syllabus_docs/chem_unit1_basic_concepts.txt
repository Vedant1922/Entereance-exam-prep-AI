SECTION 1: LAWS OF CHEMICAL COMBINATION
----------------------------------------------------------------

Five fundamental laws — JEE tests conceptual application, not just definitions.

1. Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier)
   → Total mass of reactants = Total mass of products in any chemical reaction.
   → Key: Mass is never created or destroyed, only rearranged.

2. Law of Definite Proportions (Proust)
   → A pure compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed mass ratio regardless of source or method of preparation.
   → Example: Water always has H:O ratio = 1:8 by mass. Always.

3. Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton)
   → When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole number ratios.
   → Classic JEE Example: CO and CO2 — oxygen combining with fixed carbon = 16:32 = 1:2 ratio.

4. Law of Gaseous Volumes (Gay-Lussac)
   → Gases react and produce in simple whole number ratios by volume at same T and P.
   → Example: H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl means 1 vol : 1 vol : 2 vol.

5. Avogadro's Law
   → Equal volumes of all gases at same temperature and pressure contain equal number of molecules.
   → This is why 1 mole of any gas = 22.4 L at STP.

JEE TRAP: Students confuse Law of Definite Proportions (same compound, fixed ratio) with Law of Multiple Proportions (same two elements, different compounds). Always read the question carefully.

SECTION 2: ATOMIC MASS & MOLECULAR MASS
----------------------------------------------------------------

Atomic Mass:
→ Mass of an atom relative to 1/12th mass of Carbon-12.
→ Carbon-12 is the standard reference. Its mass = exactly 12 u (unified atomic mass units).
→ 1 u = 1.66 × 10^-24 g

Important atomic masses to memorize for JEE:
H=1, C=12, N=14, O=16, Na=23, Mg=24, Al=27, S=32, Cl=35.5, K=39, Ca=40, Fe=56, Cu=63.5, Zn=65, Br=80, Ag=108, I=127, Ba=137

Molecular Mass:
→ Sum of atomic masses of all atoms in one molecule of the compound.
→ Example: H2SO4 = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98 g/mol

Equivalent Mass (not always in JEE Mains but in Advanced):
→ Equivalent mass = Molecular mass / n-factor

----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: MOLE CONCEPT — THE HEART OF THIS CHAPTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

1 mole = 6.022 × 10^23 entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons — whatever is specified)
This number is Avogadro's Number (NA) = 6.022 × 10^23 mol^-1

The mole is a counting unit, exactly like a "dozen" = 12. A mole just = 6.022 × 10^23.

KEY RELATIONSHIPS (these are the conversion bridge — master these):

   Moles = Given mass (g) / Molar mass (g/mol)

   Number of particles = Moles × NA
   Number of particles = Moles × 6.022 × 10^23

   Volume at STP (for gases only) = Moles × 22.4 L
   [STP = 0°C and 1 atm. IMPORTANT: Some JEE questions use new STP = 0°C, 1 bar → volume ≈ 22.7 L, but unless specified, use 22.4 L]

   Moles = Volume at STP / 22.4

Molar Mass:
→ Mass of 1 mole of a substance in grams.
→ Numerically equal to molecular mass but in g/mol instead of u.
→ Example: Molar mass of H2O = 18 g/mol, so 1 mole of water weighs 18 g.

JEE CRITICAL POINT: Mole concept is the bridge between the microscopic world (atoms) and the macroscopic world (grams you can measure in a lab). Every stoichiometry problem passes through moles. Never try to work directly in grams.

----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 4: PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION
----------------------------------------------------------------

% by mass of element X in compound = (Mass of X in 1 mole of compound / Molar mass of compound) × 100

Example: % of O in H2O = (16/18) × 100 = 88.9%

JEE Application: Given % composition → find empirical formula (see Section 5)

----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 5: EMPIRICAL AND MOLECULAR FORMULA
----------------------------------------------------------------

Empirical Formula = Simplest whole number ratio of atoms in a compound.
Molecular Formula = Actual number of atoms in one molecule of the compound.

Relationship:
   Molecular Formula = n × (Empirical Formula)
   where n = Molecular Mass / Empirical Formula Mass
   n must always be a positive whole number (1, 2, 3...)

STANDARD METHOD to find Empirical Formula from % composition:

Step 1: Assume 100 g of compound → % values become gram values directly.
Step 2: Convert grams of each element to moles: moles = mass / atomic mass
Step 3: Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value to get ratios.
Step 4: If ratios are not whole numbers, multiply all by smallest integer to make them whole.
Step 5: Write empirical formula from the whole number ratios.

JEE COMMON TRAP: A ratio of 1.5 → multiply everything by 2. A ratio of 1.33 → multiply by 3. A ratio of 1.25 → multiply by 4. Students often round 1.5 to 2 and get wrong empirical formula.

Example: Compound has 40% C, 6.67% H, 53.33% O
→ Moles: C = 40/12 = 3.33, H = 6.67/1 = 6.67, O = 53.33/16 = 3.33
→ Ratio: C = 3.33/3.33 = 1, H = 6.67/3.33 = 2, O = 3.33/3.33 = 1
→ Empirical formula: CH2O (glucose, fructose, acetic acid all share this empirical formula)

----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 6: CHEMICAL EQUATIONS & STOICHIOMETRY
----------------------------------------------------------------

A balanced chemical equation must satisfy:
→ Law of conservation of mass (atoms balanced on both sides)
→ Law of conservation of charge (for ionic equations)

Mole Ratio from Balanced Equation:
→ Coefficients in a balanced equation represent mole ratios directly.
→ Example: N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3
  This means: 1 mole N2 reacts with 3 moles H2 to give 2 moles NH3.
  So mole ratio N2 : H2 : NH3 = 1 : 3 : 2

LIMITING REAGENT (Limiting Reactant):
→ The reactant that is completely consumed first in a reaction. It limits how much product forms.
→ All yield calculations are based on the limiting reagent, NOT the excess reagent.

How to identify Limiting Reagent:
Step 1: Convert all given quantities to moles.
Step 2: Divide moles of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient from balanced equation.
Step 3: Whichever gives the SMALLER value is the limiting reagent.

Example: 3g H2 and 29g O2 react to form water (2H2 + O2 → 2H2O)
→ Moles H2 = 3/2 = 1.5, Moles O2 = 29/32 = 0.906
→ H2/coefficient = 1.5/2 = 0.75, O2/coefficient = 0.906/1 = 0.906
→ H2 gives smaller value → H2 is the limiting reagent
→ Moles of H2O formed = 1.5 mol (same ratio as H2, coefficient ratio 2:2 = 1:1)

Theoretical Yield vs Actual Yield:
→ Theoretical yield = calculated from limiting reagent using stoichiometry
→ % Yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100

JEE TRAP: Never calculate yield based on the excess reagent. Students who don't identify limiting reagent correctly lose marks on these questions every year.

----------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 7: JEE-SPECIFIC PATTERNS (WHAT ACTUALLY GETS ASKED)
----------------------------------------------------------------

HIGH FREQUENCY QUESTION TYPES in JEE Mains:
1. Given mass → find number of atoms/molecules (mole conversion)
2. Given % composition → find empirical + molecular formula (n-factor calculation)
3. Limiting reagent identification → find moles/mass of product formed
4. Verify or apply laws of chemical combination with numerical data
5. Mole → volume at STP → number of molecules chain conversions

CRITICAL EXCEPTIONS AND TRAPS JEE EXPLOITS:
→ 22.4 L/mol applies ONLY to gases at STP (0°C, 1 atm). Never apply to liquids or solids.
→ When the question says "at NTP" (25°C, 1 atm) → molar volume ≈ 24.4 L/mol, NOT 22.4 L/mol.
→ Avogadro's number applies to ANY particles — atoms, molecules, ions, formula units. Read carefully what entity is being counted.
→ Empirical formula can be the same as molecular formula (n=1). Example: CH2O can be empirical (formaldehyde HCHO has molecular formula CH2O, n=1).
→ Two different compounds can have the same empirical formula: glucose (C6H12O6) and acetic acid (CH3COOH) both have empirical formula CH2O.

OUT OF JEE SCOPE — DO NOT TEACH:
→ Advanced nuclear mass calculations
→ Quantum mechanical definitions of atomic mass
→ Detailed isotope abundance calculations beyond simple weighted average

SECTION 8: KEY FORMULAS SUMMARY
----------------------------------------------------------------

Moles = mass(g) / Molar mass(g/mol)
Moles = Number of particles / NA
Moles = Volume at STP(L) / 22.4
Number of particles = moles × 6.022 × 10^23
Mass = moles × Molar mass
Volume at STP = moles × 22.4 L
% composition = (mass of element in 1 mol / molar mass of compound) × 100
n (for molecular formula) = Molecular mass / Empirical formula mass
% yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100
NA = 6.022 × 10^23 mol^-1
1 u = 1.66 × 10^-24 g
Molar volume of ideal gas at STP = 22.4 L/mol
